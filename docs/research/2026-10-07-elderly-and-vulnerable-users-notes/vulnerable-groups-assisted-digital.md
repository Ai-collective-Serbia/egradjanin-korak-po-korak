# Digital inclusion of socially vulnerable groups in e-government, and the "assisted digital" / intermediary models that work for them

Scope note: international evidence only (Serbia and the Western Balkans are covered by another researcher). Every finding is tied to "eGrađanin korak po korak": a zero-cost static Astro site on GitHub Pages, phone-first, no backend, built as a graph of `step` / `question` / `card` / `end` screens in Serbian Cyrillic Markdown with screenshots. Existing screens that the findings touch: `welcome`, `what-you-need` (asks for "папир и оловку, да запишете лозинку"), `switch-tabs` (explains "картица" = browser tab with a paper metaphor), `prepare-id-photos` (asks the user to photograph both sides of the ID card), `counter-card` (large text to show at a counter), `help-account` (ends with "Неко од породице или комшија може да вам помогне" and "Налог можете отворити и на шалтеру"), `email-missing`, `help-technical`. Today is 2026-10-07; every source is dated.

## 1. How many people cannot complete government online tasks alone, and which support channels close the gap?

### Takeaway
Across the UK and EU, roughly one in five adults cannot use government digital services without help, and the share is far higher among older, low-income, low-education and disabled people. The channels that close the gap are human: family "proxy users", community volunteers ("digital champions"), libraries, and in-person counters (for example the Post Office identity check for GOV.UK One Login), backed by offline guidance. These intermediaries, not the end user, are the realistic first audience for a guide like this one.

### Cited Findings
- 19% of a nationally representative UK sample (over 2,000 respondents) cannot access government digital services without assistance; 58% reported login difficulties; 26% frequently hit problems with online public services. The report recommends expanding offline guidance and alternative support pathways, more funding for voluntary and community sector help, more visible helplines, and testing with digitally hesitant people. It also found 18 to 34 year olds facing barriers (low confidence, unreliable internet, device limits). Digital Poverty Alliance, *Accessibility of Government Services*, 29 June 2026 — [Public Sector Executive summary](https://www.publicsectorexecutive.com/articles/digital-government-services-still-out-reach-many-new-report-finds)
- UK Digital Inclusion Action Plan: First Steps (26 Feb 2025): 3.8 million people (7%) lack essential digital skills for life; about 1.6 million live largely offline; around 25% of the population are at the lowest level of digital capability; 4% of households have no laptop, PC or tablet and 2% no mobile phone; 8% of broadband and 6% of mobile customers report affordability problems (Oct 2024). The plan endorses frontline workers and "trusted members of the community to give informal help", libraries as delivery points (Manchester libraries giving free Wi-Fi, training and assisted access to the NHS App), and notes the Post Office in-person identity route for GOV.UK One Login used by over 30,000 people — [GOV.UK policy paper](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/digital-inclusion-action-plan-first-steps/digital-inclusion-action-plan-first-steps)
- GOV.UK Service Manual (page updated 18 Dec 2025): a user may need assisted digital support if they lack "trust in your service or the internet", confidence, access, digital skills, or motivation. Support is delivered by phone, in person, sometimes webchat, using in-house staff or "private, voluntary or other public sector organisations". Services do not have to support professional intermediaries (for example accountants) who are not the end user — [GOV.UK Service Manual: assisted digital](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/helping-people-to-use-your-service/assisted-digital-support-introduction)
- GOV.UK One Login offers in-person identity checks at Post Office branches for people who cannot use the app or browser route; the branch scans photo ID and takes a photo of the person. One Login names "no photographic documentation" as a barrier and is building a route for people without photo ID — [Post Office press release](https://www.mynewsdesk.com/uk/post-office/pressreleases/post-office-secures-new-government-contract-for-in-person-identity-checks-3269708); [GOV.UK: proving your identity](https://www.gov.uk/using-your-gov-uk-one-login/proving-your-identity)
- Age UK (2 Apr 2024): 4.7 million over-65s in England (more than 1 in 3) lack the basic skills to use the internet successfully; 2.3 million do not use it at all, nearly half of them 75+. The basic skills they lack include turning on a device and entering login details, opening a browser, connecting to Wi-Fi, and keeping and changing passwords. Older people describe feeling "really, really, embarrassed" asking for help — [Age UK press release](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/latest-press/articles/2024/more-than-1-in-3-over-65s-4.7-million-lack-the-basic-skills-to-use-the-internet-successfully/)
- Age UK: only 1.6 million over-65s in England are fully digitally included, 1.4 million are excluded, and 7.9 million sit "in the middle" of a spectrum; 64% of over-60s said family and friends help them with online services at least some of the time, and older people said reliance on others undermines independence and self-esteem — [Age UK press release on the digital inclusion spectrum](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/latest-press/articles/age-uk-unveils-ground-breaking-analysis-of-where-our-older-population-is-on-the-digital-inclusion-spectrum-and-calls-on-the-government-to-get-digital-done-right/) (undated on the page; reported in UK press 2023)
- Ofcom defines "proxy use" as asking someone else to use the internet on your behalf; among non-users aged 65+, 25% have someone else go online for them; 60% of people without home internet had asked someone to do something online for them in the past year. Proxies are usually adult children, but also carers, social workers and other public-facing professionals — [Ofcom Internet use and attitudes bulletin 2017](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/105507/internet-use-attitudes-bulletin-2017.pdf) (older than 2018; I could not find the same proxy figure in Ofcom's 2024 report)
- EU: only 55.6% of adults had at least basic digital skills (2021 baseline) against the Digital Decade 2030 target of 80%; JRC projects about 68.5% by 2030. Lower education, older age, disability, ethnic discrimination, unemployment and manual occupations raise the risk of below-basic skills; among 16 to 24 year olds with low education, 43% lack basic skills — [JRC news, 5 Mar 2025](https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/jrc-news-and-updates/how-reach-eu-target-80-adults-basic-digital-skills-2030-2025-03-05_en)
- UN DESA / Secretary-General: in Europe only 67% of 55 to 74 year olds use the internet weekly versus over 90% of 16 to 54 year olds, and only half as many women aged 55 to 74 have basic digital skills as younger people — [UN DESA, Aug 2022](https://www.un.org/development/desa/ageing/news/2022/08/un-chief-calls-for-a-rights-based-approach-and-a-life-cycle-perspective-to-ageing); [UN report A/77/134](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/3983524/files/A_77_134-EN.pdf)
- EU Fundamental Rights Agency (via AGE Platform Europe, 6 Nov 2023) recommends guaranteeing offline access to public services such as pensions and benefits, involving older people in the design of digital public services, and peer and intergenerational learning — [AGE Platform Europe](https://age-platform.eu/ensuring-access-to-public-services-for-older-people-in-digital-societies)
- Good Things Foundation calls "Digital Champions", usually volunteers, the cornerstone of digital inclusion: they run drop-in help sessions, courses and one-to-one support — [Good Things Foundation, 2025](https://www.goodthingsfoundation.org/discover/digital-inclusion-insights/digital-inclusion-insights-2025/digital-champions-vital-to-digital-inclusion-delivery)

### Inferences
- The guide's realistic first reader is often a helper: an adult child, a neighbour, a post or municipal clerk, a librarian, or a Red Cross or NGO volunteer. The elderly end user may only see it on the helper's phone, or as a printout. The material has to work for two readers at once: someone who reads it and does the task, and someone who explains it to another person.
- The UK's own answer for identity verification (One Login) is a staffed in-person route at the Post Office. The repo's existing `counter-card` and `help-account` → counter path follow the same pattern; it should be presented as a normal route, not a failure.
- "One in five cannot do it alone" is a UK figure. Applying it to Serbia is an assumption; the Serbia researcher should supply local numbers.

### Gaps
- I did not find an OECD Digital Government Index figure for the share of citizens who cannot complete tasks alone. The OECD reviews I could reach discuss multichannel delivery only in general terms.
- No EU DESI or Digital Decade breakdown of e-government use by income quintile was retrieved; the JRC source covers skills, not completed government transactions.
- I found no quantitative evaluation that compares completion rates across channels (proxy, volunteer, counter).

## 2. Design guidance for low-literacy readers, and evidence on read-aloud / text-to-speech

### Takeaway
Low-literacy readers read word by word, take text literally, stop at the first plausible answer, and skip dense blocks. Put the action first, keep pages short, and write at roughly a 6th to 8th grade level. Read-aloud has moderate evidence for people with reading disabilities (meta-analysis d = 0.35, mostly students) and mixed evidence for adults in general; slower speech rates help.

### Cited Findings
- Users with limited literacy read slowly, reread words and buttons, and may skip sections or start mid-paragraph. They cannot scan; they read word for word, interpret literally, and stop once they find a plausible answer. They have more trouble with short-term and working memory. Dense "walls of words" make them skip to the next link. Recommendations: 6th grade reading level on home and landing pages, 8th grade elsewhere; put the main point at the very top — [US ODPHP, Health Literacy Online, "What we know about users with limited literacy skills" (2016 edition)](https://odphp.health.gov/healthliteracyonline/2016/what-we-know/full) (pre-2018 but still the main US federal guide)
- Inclusion Europe's *Information for all* European easy-to-read standards have sections for written, electronic, video and audio information and a key-rules checklist, and come in many languages — [Inclusion Europe](https://www.inclusion-europe.eu/easy-to-read-standards-guidelines/); [PDF](https://www.inclusion-europe.eu/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/EN_Information_for_all.pdf) (standards from about 2009, page live in 2026; I did not confirm whether a Serbian version exists)
- Meta-analysis (Wood, Moxley, Tighe, Wagner, *Journal of Learning Disabilities* 51(1), 2018, online 2017): text-to-speech and read-aloud tools improved reading comprehension for students with reading disabilities, average weighted effect d = 0.35 (95% CI 0.14 to 0.56); more studies are needed on moderators — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5494021/)
- Adults with reading difficulties (n = 4, alternating treatments design): slower text-to-speech rates gave slightly better comprehension and fluency, although participants preferred faster speech — Coleman et al., 2014 — [LDMJ](https://js.sagamorepub.com/index.php/ldmj/article/view/5278) (small, pre-2018)
- Dual visual-and-audio presentation improved comprehension for 12 to 14 year old boys but not girls (n = 215, Spain); the authors suggest it may particularly help lower performers — Alvarez-Alonso et al., *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2021 — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.574685/full). The same literature reports worse adult comprehension with dual modality for some technical documents, so the evidence is mixed.
- The German LACILLA project is testing whether combined audio and text helps adults with low literacy (results not found) — [DIE Bonn](https://die-bonn.de/id/42055/about/html?lang=en)
- Older adults in two 2025 pilot studies preferred minimal text with images, high contrast, enlarged fonts, colour-coding, and "figure-of-speech" (metaphor) language for new tasks — Kellenberger, Leidich, Balasubramaniam, 2025 — [SCITEPRESS](https://www.scitepress.org/publishedPapers/2025/132990/pdf/index.html)
- Age UK's list of basic skills older people lack includes "opening a browser" (2024), which is evidence that words such as browser, tab and app cannot be assumed — [Age UK](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/latest-press/articles/2024/more-than-1-in-3-over-65s-4.7-million-lack-the-basic-skills-to-use-the-internet-successfully/)

### Inferences
- The repo's writing rules (short sentences, one action per step, numbered lists, bold only the thing to tap) already match the low-literacy evidence. Missing: an explicit rule to put the single action in the first line of each body, and a cap on body length per screen, because readers stop early.
- `switch-tabs` uses a paper metaphor ("два листа папира") for tabs, which is what the 2025 pilots recommend. The same approach should apply to every unavoidable technical word: explain it once with a physical metaphor, then use the same word every time.
- Read-aloud is cheap to add on a static site through the browser's built-in speech engine, but the evidence is moderate and mostly from students. Treat it as an optional aid, default to a slow rate, and test it with real users first.

### Gaps
- I found no study on Serbian or other South Slavic text-to-speech voice quality in phone browsers, or on read-aloud specifically in e-government guides.
- I found no rigorous evidence on icons for low-literacy e-government users. ODPHP and Inclusion Europe recommend supporting images, but I found no outcome data on icons.
- I did not retrieve EU Clear Writing or plainlanguage.gov texts directly. Their guidance is widely known (short sentences, active voice, everyday words) but is not cited here.

## 3. Shared or borrowed devices, no personal email, no phone credit, prepaid SIMs as barriers to identity verification

### Takeaway
Lack of a personal device, a personal email or affordable data is common among the poor, women in lower-income settings and older people, and it collides with identity flows that assume one person, one phone, one inbox. Systems handle this with assisted in-person routes, alternatives to photo ID, and proxy access, not with more online steps.

### Cited Findings
- GSMA Mobile Gender Gap Report 2025: 28% of women who use mobile internet in Pakistan do so only on someone else's phone, versus 4% of men. Shared devices help women get online but limit how often they use it and what they do, and reduce privacy and independence. An entry-level handset costs 24% of a woman's monthly income in low and middle-income countries versus 12% of a man's. Literacy and digital skills are the other top barrier — [GSMA report](https://www.gsma.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/The-Mobile-Gender-Gap-Report-2025.pdf); [Arab News summary](https://www.arabnews.pk/node/2649428/pakistan)
- UK: 2% of households have no mobile phone and 4% no computer or tablet; 6% of mobile customers struggle to afford service (Oct 2024). The plan responds with a device donation and redistribution scheme — [Digital Inclusion Action Plan, Feb 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/digital-inclusion-action-plan-first-steps/digital-inclusion-action-plan-first-steps)
- World Bank ID4D: up to 1 billion people lack ID; even well-meant ID systems exclude the poor, remote residents, women and girls, migrants and refugees, who often lack smartphones, computers or broadband. The Mission Billion Challenge sought ways for the most vulnerable to register for and use digital IDs safely — [World Bank press release, 27 May 2020](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2020/05/27/world-banks-mission-billion-challenge-calls-for-innovation-to-strengthen-inclusion-and-empower-the-worlds-invisible-billion)
- Civil society (open letter, Sep 2022) asked the World Bank to stop backing digital ID systems that enable "surveillance, exclusion, and discrimination", citing centralised biometric models — [Access Now](https://www.accessnow.org/world-bank-digital-id-systems/)
- GOV.UK One Login: people without the required photo ID can verify with bank details and security questions, or in person at the Post Office; a route for people without photo ID is in development — [Post Office / UKAuthority](https://www.ukauthority.com/articles/post-office-to-provide-face-to-face-identity-checks-for-one-login/); [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/using-your-gov-uk-one-login/proving-your-identity)
- 58% of UK respondents report login problems with government services (DPA, June 2026) — [Public Sector Executive](https://www.publicsectorexecutive.com/articles/digital-government-services-still-out-reach-many-new-report-finds)

### Inferences
- In the repo's flow, a borrowed phone creates three risks: (a) ID card photos from `prepare-id-photos` stay in the helper's gallery; (b) the eUprava email may be created on, and stay logged in to, someone else's device; (c) the ConsentID app (`install-consentid`, `activate-consentid`) is bound to a phone, so a borrowed phone may not work as a lasting second factor. The guide should say plainly which steps need the user's own phone and which can be done on anyone's. (Exact eUprava rules on this are outside my scope; the Serbia researcher should confirm them.)
- An email address is a hard requirement (`what-you-need`, `create-email`). People without one may create it with a helper and then forget it. The guide should prompt them to write down the address as well as the password, on paper they keep.
- No phone credit or data: a static page that loads fast and can be printed or saved helps people who borrow Wi-Fi at a library or post office.

### Gaps
- No source found on prepaid SIM churn (changing numbers) breaking SMS-based recovery in European e-government; the evidence is from LMIC mobile research.
- No direct evidence on Roma, refugee or displaced users and e-government identity flows was retrieved in this pass (ID4D names migrants and refugees only in general terms).

## 4. Proxy and trusted-intermediary models: how they operate safely (consent, privacy, ID photos, passwords)

### Takeaway
Formal proxy schemes (NHS App proxy access) separate the helper's identity from the user's, require consent, and check for safeguarding risk. Volunteer-helper guidance converges on a few rules: ask consent first, the user types their own password, the helper never keeps passwords, documents or personal data, record what was done, and teach rather than do. Informal family help, the most common kind, has no such rules unless someone gives them to the helper.

### Cited Findings
- NHS proxy access: a trusted person (proxy) can act for a patient online through their own NHS App or GP account, for example ordering prescriptions or booking appointments. GP practices decide whether proxy access is "safe, necessary and relevant", get the patient's consent where needed, and are warned that proxy access "could put some vulnerable patients at risk", so benefits must be balanced against risks — [NHS England Digital, proxy application service (archived May 2026)](https://tnaqa.mirrorweb.com/ukgwa/20260506071410mp_/https://digital.nhs.uk/services/proxy-application-service); [North Central London ICB](https://gps.northcentrallondon.icb.nhs.uk/digital-tools-supporting-gp-access/nhs-app/proxy-access)
- Digital champion safeguarding guidance: get consent before giving digital support, as for face-to-face support; consider a learner consent form; never share passwords; seeing passwords, keeping hold of data and sharing personal details are safeguarding scenarios; keep only necessary learner information in password-protected files; champions may support NHS services, online banking and personal-data forms and need safeguarding training — [Digital Unite, "Safely does it" (2021)](https://www.digitalunite.com/sites/default/files/2021-03/Safely%20does%20it%2023%20March.pdf); [Digital Unite, "10 top tips" (15 Apr 2021)](https://www.digitalunite.com/news-and-views/10-top-tips-keeping-remote-digital-skills-support-safe); [SCVO digital privacy](https://scvo.scot/support/digital/privacy/digital); [Good Things Foundation safeguarding for digital champions (2024 event)](https://www.dev.goodthingsfoundation.org/discover/events/events-public-2024/safeguarding-for-digital-champions). Caveat: my summary of the "Safely does it" PDF came from an automated extraction that I could not check word for word; the points above match the search snippets.
- Healthwatch Islington, quoted by Digital Unite: champions get a separate SIM and a monitored central email; only first name and contact details are shared with champions; learners asking champions to contact someone on their behalf "can raise other issues" — [Digital Unite, 15 Apr 2021](https://www.digitalunite.com/news-and-views/10-top-tips-keeping-remote-digital-skills-support-safe)
- GOV.UK does not require services to give assisted digital support to intermediaries who are not the end user, which keeps the end user as the account holder — [GOV.UK Service Manual](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/helping-people-to-use-your-service/assisted-digital-support-introduction)
- Older people say relying on others undermines independence and self-esteem (Age UK) — [Age UK](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/latest-press/articles/age-uk-unveils-ground-breaking-analysis-of-where-our-older-population-is-on-the-digital-inclusion-spectrum-and-calls-on-the-government-to-get-digital-done-right/)
- Proxy help happens mostly within families, but also through carers and social workers (Ofcom 2017) — [Ofcom](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/105507/internet-use-attitudes-bulletin-2017.pdf)

### Inferences
- An eUprava/eID account is a legal identity, not a convenience login, and there is no formal proxy mechanism for it in this guide's scope. A helper must therefore help the user do it themselves, not open the account for them. The guide should say so in plain words, addressed to the helper.
- Concrete helper rules that follow from the safeguarding material: the user types the password (or the helper looks away); the password goes on the user's own paper, not in the helper's notes or phone; ID card photos taken on a helper's phone are deleted after upload, including from "recently deleted" and cloud backups; the helper logs out of the user's email on their device.
- This contradicts nothing in the repo, but `what-you-need` tells users to write the password on paper. That suits this audience (memory) but should add "keep it at home, do not give it to anyone, including the person helping you". This is an inference: I found no source that weighs written-down passwords for older users.

### Gaps
- No evaluation found of informal family proxy harms (financial abuse through e-government accounts). The NHS guidance flags risk but gives no rates.
- No international model found for a proxy or mandate for a national eID account (as opposed to health records); worth asking the Serbia researcher whether eUprava has any authorisation or "punomoćje" feature.

## 5. Printable or offline companions (checklists, "bring this to the counter" cards) and completion

### Takeaway
There is qualitative but no rigorous quantitative evidence: older learners prefer and keep printed, task-specific, step-by-step guides with screenshots, which reduce anxiety; programmes hand out printed copies; the 2026 UK study recommends more offline guidance. I found no controlled trial linking a printed companion to higher completion.

### Cited Findings
- Two 2025 pilot studies (n = 4 and n = 7, ages 60s to 80s): participants preferred tangible resources ("I found myself printing off the instructions so I have them to keep...at any point I can refer to a page"). Device-specific, task-oriented printed guides with visuals worked best; holding physical material reduced anxiety and increased motivation; the authors recommend community training sessions and ongoing peer support — Kellenberger et al., 2025 — [SCITEPRESS](https://www.scitepress.org/publishedPapers/2025/132990/pdf/index.html)
- A gerontechnology study on teaching older adults to use tablets reports that printed handouts aid memory, and that older adults often prefer printed resources before moving to digital ones — [Gerontechnology](https://journal.gerontechnology.org/currentIssueContent.aspx?aid=2209) (date not confirmed)
- The STEHL (Smart Tablet Education for Healthy Living) toolkit gives participants printed copies of the step-by-step handbook to use during and after workshops — [NIU STEHL toolkit](https://niu.edu/stehl/_files/stehl-toolkit.docx) (date not confirmed)
- The DPA (June 2026) recommends that public bodies "expand offline guidance and alternative support pathways" — [Public Sector Executive](https://www.publicsectorexecutive.com/articles/digital-government-services-still-out-reach-many-new-report-finds)
- A systematic review of digital literacy in lifelong learning for older adults found that step-by-step instruction and careful pacing improve learning — [HKMU](https://scholars.hkmu.edu.hk/en/publications/a-systematic-review-of-digital-literacy-in-lifelong-learning-for-/) (year not confirmed)

### Inferences
- Paper fits this project: it costs nothing, needs no backend, and serves both the helper (a script) and the user (something to keep). Two printable artifacts follow: (a) a one-page "what you need and what you will do" checklist matching `what-you-need`, with blanks for the email address and a reminder where the password sheet is kept; (b) the existing `counter-card`, which already is a "bring this to the counter" card and should print cleanly on A4 or A5.
- Because effects on completion are unproven, the team should treat printables as a hypothesis and observe them in a small test with real helpers.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found that measures completion of an online government task with and without a printed companion.
- No evidence found on the "show this screen at the counter" pattern specifically (the repo's `card` type). It is analogous to the One Login Post Office route and health "patient passports", but no outcome data was retrieved.

## 6. Practical guidance from UNDP, UNFPA, the Digital Public Goods Alliance and the UN on inclusive digital public services and ageing

### Takeaway
UN-system material is mostly principles and safeguards (rights-based, life-course, offline alternatives, participation of older people) rather than practical design checklists. The most actionable items are the Universal DPI Safeguards Framework (UNDP with the UN Tech Envoy) and calls to involve older people in design. I found no DPGA design or evaluation checklist on ageing.

### Cited Findings
- The UN released the Universal DPI Safeguards Framework, "actionable guidelines" for digital public infrastructure in the public interest, developed by the Office of the Secretary-General's Envoy on Technology and UNDP — [UNDP press release](https://www.undp.org/press-releases/un-releases-universal-dpi-safeguards-framework-promote-safe-and-inclusive-digital-public-infrastructure) (page returned HTTP 403; content from search snippet; release date not confirmed, believed 2024)
- The UN Secretary-General calls for a rights-based, life-cycle approach to ageing in the digital space and warns that older persons, especially older women, risk being left behind — [UN DESA, Aug 2022](https://www.un.org/development/desa/ageing/news/2022/08/un-chief-calls-for-a-rights-based-approach-and-a-life-cycle-perspective-to-ageing); [A/77/134](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/3983524/files/A_77_134-EN.pdf)
- UNFPA China ran a youth innovation challenge on "age-friendly design" for digital products to reduce exclusion of older persons, especially women and people with disabilities — [UNFPA China RFP](https://china.unfpa.org/en/rfp/23061501) (2023)
- An international standard exists, *Ageing societies — General requirements and guidelines for an ageing-inclusive digital economy* (ISO 25556, listed by the Senegalese standards body) — [ASN listing](https://asn.sn/en/node/56750) (not read in full)
- EU FRA (2023): involve older people in designing digital public services; guarantee offline access — [AGE Platform Europe](https://age-platform.eu/ensuring-access-to-public-services-for-older-people-in-digital-societies)

### Inferences
- For this repo, the UN material mainly justifies process choices: keep an offline route visible (counter), involve older users and helpers in testing, and avoid ageist framing. These can be cited in a funding or partnership pitch (for example to UNDP Serbia or UNFPA Serbia, which the Serbia researcher may cover).

### Gaps
- I found no Digital Public Goods Alliance publication with practical guidance on ageing or assisted digital; DPGA registry criteria (open licence, privacy, do-no-harm) may still matter if the team wants the guide listed as a digital public good. Not verified in this pass.
- UNESCO Media and Information Literacy material was not retrieved.
- I did not read the full Universal DPI Safeguards Framework, so I cannot say whether it covers assisted channels in detail.

## 7. Trust: fear of scams, reluctance to photograph an ID card, and how guides build legitimacy

### Takeaway
Lack of trust is an official reason for needing assisted digital support (GOV.UK). Older people's fear of scams is well documented, and the public is generally reluctant to upload government ID. The evidence on what builds trust in a third-party guide is thin; the practical levers are naming the official issuing institution and official URL, stating clearly what the guide never asks for, and being introduced by a trusted person or place.

### Cited Findings
- GOV.UK lists lack of "trust in your service or the internet" as one of five reasons users need assisted digital support (Dec 2025) — [GOV.UK Service Manual](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/helping-people-to-use-your-service/assisted-digital-support-introduction)
- Age UK found that many online older people lack online safety skills, and "maintaining password security" is among the basic skills many lack (Apr 2024) — [Age UK](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/latest-press/articles/2024/more-than-1-in-3-over-65s-4.7-million-lack-the-basic-skills-to-use-the-internet-successfully/)
- In age-verification research, two-thirds of Americans said they were not comfortable uploading a government ID to social media companies; under 5% of Australian adults fully trust platforms to store their data and almost half have no trust at all. People fear identity theft and data breaches — [arXiv 2603.25695, 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.25695) (context is age assurance on private platforms, not government services)
- Older Australians' intention to keep using online platforms is affected by identity theft from scams (romance, phone and phishing scams) — [University of the Sunshine Coast, Sep 2017](https://www.unisc.edu.au/about/unisc-news/news-archive/2017/september/usc-study-to-help-older-victims-of-scams) (pre-2018)
- FRA-related material stresses helping older adults recognise common scam patterns — [AGE Platform Europe, Nov 2023](https://age-platform.eu/ensuring-access-to-public-services-for-older-people-in-digital-societies)

### Inferences
- `prepare-id-photos` asks users to photograph both sides of the ID card before they reach the official site. For a scam-wary user, a third-party site asking for ID photos looks like a scam, even though the photos never leave the phone. The screen should say explicitly that the photos are uploaded only on the official site (name the domain), that this guide never receives them, and that no one from the guide will ever call or message asking for them.
- Legitimacy for a volunteer guide comes from: (a) saying who made it and that it is unofficial, (b) linking only to named official domains (eid.gov.rs, euprava.gov.rs) and teaching users to check the address bar, (c) a "we never ask for" statement (no password, no ID photo, no JMBG, no money), and (d) being handed over by a trusted person or institution (library, post office, Red Cross). Item (d) depends on partnerships.
- An "official endorsement" badge must not be implied unless it exists. The project's rules on impersonation and the scam context both argue for a clear "unofficial, volunteer-made" label.

### Gaps
- No study found on how independent or NGO guides establish legitimacy compared with official ones, or on whether "we never ask for" statements reduce scam fear.
- No evidence found specifically on older people's reluctance to photograph their own ID for a government service; the cited data concern private platforms.
