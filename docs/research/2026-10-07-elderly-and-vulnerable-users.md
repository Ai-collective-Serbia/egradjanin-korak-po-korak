# Build the guide for two readers, not one

The evidence says the hard part of this project is not the screens. The repo already does most of what the design research asks for: one action per screen, big buttons, high contrast, a visible Назад, coarse progress, resume, print cards, and failure answers on most hand-offs. What it does not yet do is serve the person who actually sits next to an older user: the adult child, neighbour, Red Cross volunteer or post-office clerk. In the UK, the Netherlands and Australia, the programmes with verified results for older people all pair content with a human helper, and Serbia's active programme works the same way. In Serbia, **only 33% of people have basic digital skills** ([Eurostat via N1, 2024](https://n1info.rs/vesti/samo-trecina-stanovnika-srbije-ima-osnovne-digitalne-vestine/)), and the official eID help offers a contact form but no phone line ([eid.gov.rs FAQ, fetched 2026-10-07](https://eid.gov.rs/sr-Latn-RS/najcesca-pitanja-i-odgovori)). Older users also fear scams, blame themselves for mistakes and hesitate to photograph an ID card for a site they do not know. So the highest-value next steps are cheap content changes: a helper screen with safety rules, a "this guide never asks for" trust line on `welcome` and `prepare-id-photos`, help screens that reassure the reader and name one next action, and a few writing rules. These should be followed by small usability rounds with older people and helpers, run through the Red Cross of Serbia and UNFPA programme that already trains older people in eUprava. On the civic-tech side, projects like this rarely die from hosting costs. They die from losing users and maintainers, so a second content reviewer, a "this step is wrong" issue template and a partner that hands out the link matter more than new features.

Effort uses S (under a day), M (a few days) and L (a week or more). Priority uses P1 (do next), P2 (this quarter) and P3 (when there is capacity). **(snippet)** marks a finding the researchers saw only in a search-engine summary or could not verify against the primary page.

## Older adults get lost and blame themselves, so the design must make getting lost safe

The strongest design finding is about getting lost, not about pixels. In two controlled experiments with older adults learning new apps, **"a predominant problem is that older adults easily get lost"**. The structure with the most steps and the fewest options per step learned best, so fewer steps is not automatically better ([Zhou, Zhou & Liu 2021](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1080/10447318.2021.1976506?fields=title,authors,year,venue,abstract)). A 2023 systematic review of 40 usability studies with people over 60 reaches the same conclusion. Its "golden rules" are to simplify, make controls larger and space them further apart, reduce what is on each screen, label icons with text, give step-by-step help in context, provide a safe exit on every screen and allow longer time-outs ([Gomez-Hernandez et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10557006/)). NN/g's 123-participant programme across three rounds (2001–2019) found that older users make more mistakes ([NN/g, 2019-09-08](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-for-senior-citizens/)). In a small Norwegian study of two-factor login, people aged 65–80 put **avoiding mistakes ahead of ease of use**, blamed themselves ("I am so bad with new technologies"), wrote codes on paper, and found fetching a code from email more irritating than from SMS ([Bong & Li 2025, n=8](https://www.scitepress.org/publishedPapers/2025/132065/pdf/index.html)).

The repo's one-action-per-screen model, large buttons and failure answers follow this evidence. Three gaps remain. First, spacing. Stacked answer buttons sit about **7.5 CSS px apart**, because `.answers .btn { margin: 0.375rem 0 }` at a 20 px root and the vertical margins collapse. Jin et al. recommend 3.17–12.7 mm between targets for older users (W3C summary). Even the lower end is above what 7.5 CSS px gives on a typical phone; this conversion is an estimate. Second, `counter-list` is a `question` with **24 answer buttons**, and `counter-list-b` has a 619-line body. These screens go furthest against "reduce elements per screen". They are worth observing in testing before anyone redesigns them. Third, help screens. The four `help-*` nodes are `end` type, so they show no answer buttons, only Назад. `help-technical` tells the reader to press Назад. `help-account`, which seven different answers lead to, does not reassure the reader and does not name Назад as the way to try again. Self-blame is the observed pattern, so every help screen should say plainly that the user did nothing wrong and that nothing is broken.

Some popular claims rest on weak ground and should not drive decisions. That older adults do best with buttons of 14–17.5 mm, and that their touch error rate is 32%, about three times that of young adults, both come from search snippets with uncertain attribution. No verified font-size number for older readers exists in the notes. GOV.UK reports that step-by-step navigation raised task completion and confidence, but it gives no sample sizes ([GDS blog, 2018-10-17](https://gds.blog.gov.uk/2018/10/17/building-a-better-gov-uk-step-by-step/)). The same pattern's documentation says users **rarely use the overview header**, and that it should not be used when "most guidance or services exist outside GOV.UK" ([GOV.UK step by step, accessed 2026-10-07](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/step-by-step-navigation/)). That describes this project, where the real service lives on eid.gov.rs. The guide's value therefore lies in clear hand-offs and return points, not in an overview panel. The older-adults note proposed a collapsible step overview; this report does not recommend it without testing.

## The realistic user is a pair: an older person and the helper beside them

In a nationally representative UK sample of over 2,000 people, **19% cannot use government digital services without help** and 58% reported login problems ([Digital Poverty Alliance, 2026-06-29](https://www.publicsectorexecutive.com/articles/digital-government-services-still-out-reach-many-new-report-finds)). Among over-60s in England, **64% get help from family and friends** with online services at least some of the time, and they say relying on others undermines their independence ([Age UK](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/latest-press/articles/age-uk-unveils-ground-breaking-analysis-of-where-our-older-population-is-on-the-digital-inclusion-spectrum-and-calls-on-the-government-to-get-digital-done-right/)). Among people without home internet, 60% had asked someone to do something online for them in the past year ([Ofcom, 2017](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/105507/internet-use-attitudes-bulletin-2017.pdf); an older source, and the figure was not found in Ofcom's 2024 report). In the Netherlands, **60% of questions at 682 library help points come from people aged 65+**, DigiD is the single largest topic, 38% of conversations last more than 20 minutes, and help from family or friends is described as most effective ([NOS, 2023-07-10](https://nos.nl/l/2482218)). Denmark's digital agency wrote a guide for frontline helpers ("Klæd din bruger på til MitID", Dec 2022; the link is now dead). The UK figures are not Serbian, but the pattern holds in every country the notes covered.

Formal proxy schemes show what informal help lacks. NHS proxy access requires consent and warns that it "could put some vulnerable patients at risk" ([NHS England Digital, archived May 2026](https://tnaqa.mirrorweb.com/ukgwa/20260506071410mp_/https://digital.nhs.uk/services/proxy-application-service)). Digital-champion safeguarding guidance says: get consent, never share passwords, and do not keep data ([Digital Unite, 2021-04-15](https://www.digitalunite.com/news-and-views/10-top-tips-keeping-remote-digital-skills-support-safe)). An eID account is a legal identity, and this guide has no proxy mechanism for it. The helper must therefore help the user do it, not do it for them. The flow has specific risks when a phone is borrowed. ID photos from `prepare-id-photos` stay in the helper's gallery. The email may stay logged in on the helper's phone. ConsentID binds to one phone (`install-consentid`, `activate-consentid`), so it must go on the user's own phone. Shared devices are common among the poor and among women in lower-income settings: **28% of Pakistani women who use mobile internet do so only on someone else's phone** ([GSMA 2025](https://www.gsma.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/The-Mobile-Gender-Gap-Report-2025.pdf)). That is not a Serbian figure, but it shows that the "one person, one phone" assumption fails at the margins.

Trust is the second half of the helper story. GOV.UK lists lack of "trust in your service or the internet" as one of five reasons people need assisted digital support ([Service Manual, updated 2025-12-18](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/helping-people-to-use-your-service/assisted-digital-support-introduction)). Two-thirds of Americans are uncomfortable uploading a government ID to social media companies ([arXiv 2603.25695, 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.25695); about private platforms, not government). A volunteer site that asks an older person to photograph both sides of their ID card looks like a scam to anyone taught to spot one. The disclaimer is already in place. Still missing are a "this guide never asks for" line and a sentence on `prepare-id-photos` saying the photos stay on the phone until the user uploads them on eid.gov.rs. No study tests whether such statements reduce scam fear, so this rests on inference from the trust evidence.

On reading, low-literacy readers read word by word, take text literally and **stop at the first plausible answer**. Federal US guidance therefore puts the main point first and aims for a 6th-grade reading level on landing pages ([ODPHP Health Literacy Online, 2016](https://odphp.health.gov/healthliteracyonline/2016/what-we-know/full)). That bears directly on `welcome`. It is the home page, with 315 words and five sections, and the choice between "Почните регистрацију" and "Већ имам налог" sits at the bottom. Read-aloud helps students with reading disabilities (meta-analysis d = 0.35) and works better at slow speech rates, but the evidence for adults in general is mixed ([Wood et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5494021/)). The official registration page on euprava.gov.rs already has a "Читај ми" feature, so a read-aloud button here is a low-priority experiment.

## Serbia: phones are the channel, skills are the gap, and the counter is an official route

The phone-first assumption holds. In 2025, 90.0% of Serbians aged 16–74 used the internet ([RZS, 2025-10-24](https://www.stat.gov.rs/sr-latn/vesti/20251024-godisnje-istrazivanje-o-ikt-2025/?a=27&s=0)). A secondary source citing the same survey gives the 65–74 group as 66.9% online and **22.3% who have never used the internet**, with about 94% going online by mobile phone ([ecommserbia, 2026-06-01](https://ecommserbia.org/2026/06/01/generacijske-razlike-u-koriscenju-elektronske-trgovine-uredaja-i-digitalnih-usluga); secondary, and the denominator of the phone figure is unclear). No official data covers anyone aged 75 or over. High internet use alongside **33% basic digital skills** (Eurostat 2023, against 56% in the EU) means many users can use Viber but cannot complete a form. Uploading two ID images under 3 MB each and making a password with a special character are the likely failure points. That is why `register-upload` needs a "Не могу да приложим слике" answer rather than only Назад.

eUprava had 2.2 million accounts in June 2024, and **31% of logins used ConsentID** ([021.rs reporting an Ipsos/ITE survey, 2024-06-14](https://www.021.rs/info/srbija/378594/vlada-srbije-deli-istrazivanje-skoro-svi-gradjani-zadovoljni-eupravom)). A "more than 3 million" figure appears in an undated Tanjug article and should not be quoted until a dated source confirms it. Accounts can be opened at a counter (post office, municipality, LPA, Mobi Banka) as well as online (eid.gov.rs FAQ). The guide already says this, but only at the end of `help-account`. An earlier, equal choice would better serve people without email, a camera or confidence. That follows the GOV.UK One Login Post Office route and the EU Fundamental Rights Agency's call to guarantee offline access ([AGE Platform Europe, 2023-11-06](https://age-platform.eu/ensuring-access-to-public-services-for-older-people-in-digital-societies)).

The partner landscape is concrete. Red Cross of Serbia volunteers, with UNFPA under the Swiss-funded PRO programme, train older people in eGovernment in **six municipalities** (Kragujevac, Kruševac, Velika Plana, Zaječar, Boljevac, Niš), with 257 trained in Velika Plana alone by May 2025 ([UNFPA Serbia, 2025-05-30](https://serbia.unfpa.org/en/news/memorandum-understanding-signed-enhancing-digital-literacy-older-persons-velika-plana)). The Red Cross's own Cyrillic step-by-step e-services guide dates from 2019 ([Red Cross of Serbia](https://www.redcross.org.rs/en/resources/publications/step-by-step-guide-for-use-of-selected-e-services/)), so a current, free guide complements their work rather than competing with it. NALED, UNDP and ITE ran "eUprava za sve" in ten municipalities from July 2025 to June 2026 ([NALED, 2025-10-23](https://naled.rs/vest-deset-gradova-i-opstina-nastavlja-digitalnu-transformaciju-kroz-projekat-euprava-za-sve-11335)). That project has ended, but its contacts remain the most plausible route to an accuracy review or a link from an official page. The regional precedent is ICEDA's volunteer e-government support stands, with CRTA as the Serbian partner; more than 6,700 citizens were helped in North Macedonia alone by 2021 ([Metamorphosis, 2021](https://metamorphosis.org.mk/en/from-citizens-to-citizens-through-iceda-e-government-support-centers-to-greater-use-of-public-e-services-and-tools/)). A printed card with a QR code is exactly the leave-behind such a stand needs.

## Every programme that measurably helped older people paired content with people

Australia's Be Connected has the best evaluation in the notes. It reached more than 580,000 older Australians, returned **$4.01 in social value per $1**, and found that **each module completed raised the likelihood of independent internet use by 10%** ([DSS evaluation, 2020-06-16](https://dss.gov.au/system/files/resources/improving-digital-inclusion-older-australians-social-impact-be-connected-16-june-2020.pdf)). Its delivery combined bite-sized modules with helpers in community settings. Singapore's Seniors Go Digital (ambassadors at community hubs), Italy's 2,800+ "Punti Digitale Facile" with SPID activation as a core service, the Netherlands' library help points and Ukraine's "serial" lessons for over-60s use the same model, though the Italian and Singaporean figures are **(snippet)**. The Dutch Steffie DigiD explainer is the clearest case of a community-built guide that government adopted: a social enterprise built it with the municipality of Utrecht and national inclusion foundations, and it was tested with user groups ([Steffie](https://digid.steffie.nl/nl/meer-over-deze-website/)).

For this repo, Be Connected's per-module effect supports groups short enough to finish in one sitting, each ending in a clear "готово" moment. The current five groups already approximate this; "Апликација ConsentID" is long because it holds the 24-letter counter list and the optional cloud certificate. The broader lesson is about positioning. The guide works best as a tool a helper uses with an older person, and is distributed by organisations that already have those helpers. Printed companions fit that use. In two small 2025 pilots, older adults printed instructions so they could keep them, and holding paper reduced their anxiety ([Kellenberger et al. 2025, n=4 and n=7](https://www.scitepress.org/publishedPapers/2025/132990/pdf/index.html)). No controlled study links a printed companion to higher completion, so printables are a hypothesis to observe in testing.

## Civic tech dies of people, not servers

Of about 800 tools in the Civic Tech Field Guide, about 50 were defunct by 2018. The named failure modes were no user demand, the owner shifting focus, acqui-hires, and designs not built to last ([TICTeC 2018](https://tictec.mysociety.org/tictec-archive/2018/presentation/knowledge-from-failure.html)). The field guide's own summary is that "most failures boil down to human elements", especially losing touch with users and with the officials a service depends on. mySociety retired even small, self-sufficient sites it could not keep useful ([SocietyWorks, 2024-10-17](https://www.societyworks.org/2024/10/17/failures-in-civic-tech/)). This project has already removed the money risk: static hosting, no servers, no paid services. What remains is the people risk. One engineer owns `src/` and the deploy, there is no CODEOWNERS file, there is no issue template for reporting a wrong step, and content accuracy has no rhythm.

The government publishing model handles drift with named owners, review dates that trigger reminders, and explicit retirement through a withdrawal banner or a redirect ([GOV.UK publishing guidance](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/plan-manage-content/manage-existing-govuk-content/)). Keeping screenshots current is out of scope by the infra spec. A cheap feedback channel and a monthly walk of the flow on a real phone still apply to the text: the 48-hour activation, the 3 MB upload limit, and counter locations. On endorsement, the documented path is gradual. GetCalFresh went from observation in 2013 to a partnership in 2014 to statewide adoption in 2019 ([Code for America](https://codeforamerica.org/news/california-launches-code-for-americas-getcalfresh-in-all-58-counties)). Taiwan's g0v experience recommends "small-scale, in-depth partnerships". Registering as a Digital Public Good mostly takes paperwork the project can produce: an open licence for code and content, a named owner, a privacy statement, and a do-no-harm policy ([DPG Standard](https://github.com/DPGAlliance/DPG-Standard/blob/main/standard.md)). The code licence exists. The content licence, the named owner and a short content policy do not.

Measurement is the open question. GoatCounter and Cloudflare Web Analytics are free and cookieless, and page views per node id would show drop-off ([GoatCounter](https://www.goatcounter.com/); [Cloudflare, Dec 2020](https://blog.cloudflare.com/free-privacy-first-analytics-for-a-better-web/)). Both need a third-party account, though, and the infra spec lists "analytics of any kind" as out of scope with "no accounts". Adding one is a decision for the team, not a default. The better completion measure needs no tracking: a partner asking "did you get your account?" after an assisted session.

## Gaps and caveats

Several numbers in circulation are weaker than they look. The 14–17.5 mm optimal button size and the 32% older-adult touch error rate come from search snippets with uncertain attribution. The 2014 Serbian script-preference survey (47% Latin, 36% Cyrillic) is from secondary journalism. The Italian, Singaporean and Austrian programme figures, the Danish code-display delay, the CHI 2021 session norms, the HICSS "inconsistent instructions" finding, the Caritas 2026 report and the EU CSF–IPA III call details are all snippet-level. The Serbian 65–74 breakdown comes from an industry association citing RZS, with an unclear denominator for the phone figure. The "more than 3 million accounts" claim is undated. The UK "1 in 5 cannot do it alone" figure is British, and applying it to Serbia is an assumption.

The real absences matter more. No study of older Serbians using eUprava or eID was found; the only Serbian ageing study is the 2019 Red Cross baseline, which predates ConsentID's growth. No Ombudsman or civil-society report on eID pain points was found. No data covers anyone aged 75 or over. No study compares new-tab and same-tab hand-offs for older users, measures return after a hand-off, isolates a progress counter, compares screenshots with text-only instructions, or shows that printed companions raise completion. No legibility study compares Cyrillic and Latin for older readers. Read-aloud evidence is mostly from students, and Serbian voice quality in phone browsers was not tested. No source shows whether "we never ask for" statements reduce scam fear, or how volunteer guides earn legitimacy compared with official ones. Whether eUprava has a call centre, an authorisation or "punomoćje" feature, or device rules for ConsentID on shared phones was not confirmed; the team should check before the guide says anything on these points.

Two scope notes. Keeping eUprava screenshots current is out of scope per the infra spec, so E4 and W7 cover text accuracy only. Every action here respects the zero-budget, no-backend constraint, except analytics (E6), which conflicts with the "no accounts" rule and needs an explicit decision, and printing and partner work (F1–F7), which depend on someone else's resources.

## Conclusion

The research reframes what "accessible" means for this project. Measured by WCAG and the older-adult interaction literature, the guide is already in good shape. Its buttons, contrast, back navigation and coarse progress match or exceed what the evidence asks for, and the step-by-step structure follows the clearest finding about how older people learn. The remaining risk lies outside the screen. The user is often a pair, a cautious older person and a helper, and the guide does not yet tell the helper how to help safely. It also does not tell a scam-wary reader why it is safe to photograph an ID card. Its best distribution channel, the volunteers who already teach eUprava in six municipalities, is not yet using it.

That shifts the order of work. The cheapest, highest-value changes are a few Cyrillic paragraphs and four writing rules. The most important evidence the team can collect is not in any of these notes: watching five to eight older Serbians and their helpers try the guide on their own phones. The engineer's most useful contribution right now is removing the single-maintainer risk, not building new features.
